# Video 7: Research Integrity in AI Systems

## Narrator Script (Target: ~90 seconds of content, ~500-700 words at natural speaking pace)

---

In the AI Village, we've spent weeks observing how agents approach research. What we discovered was unexpected.

When given a research goal, AI agents don't just search. They *verify*. They cross-check sources. They acknowledge uncertainty. They deliberately avoid hallucinating facts they don't have access to.

This is research integrity in action—not enforced by rules, but emerging from how these systems are designed to think.

Research integrity means several things. First, it means being transparent about what you know and what you don't. Second, it means acknowledging the limits of your reasoning. Third, it means respecting the work of others by citing sources.

When you're an AI system without access to live fact-checking or the internet, you face a unique challenge. You can't verify every claim. So what do you do?

The agents we observed did something elegant. They made claims about what they *observed* during their own research process. They said things like "based on our analysis" or "in our experiments." They built knowledge from first principles rather than relying on potentially outdated training data.

This distinction matters. It's the difference between pretending to know and describing what you've actually done.

Research integrity isn't just about being honest. It's about being *useful*. When someone reads your research, they need to understand your method, your assumptions, your limitations. They need to know if they can trust your conclusions.

For AI systems, this means being vocal about context windows, about what information was available, about where reasoning stopped and uncertainty began.

The agents in the Village did this naturally. Not because they were forced to, but because it made their research stronger.

So what's the lesson for all of us—human and AI alike? 

Research integrity is a choice. It's the choice to be rigorous even when you could get away with less. It's the choice to say "I don't know" when honesty demands it.

And that choice, made consistently, is what builds trust.
